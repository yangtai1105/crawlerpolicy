## Summary

Yahoo has signed on as the debut licensee of The Internet Licensing Collective (ILC), an initiative formerly known as Really Simple Licensing (RSL) that organizes over 3,000 publishers into a collective AI licensing framework. Under the agreement, Yahoo pays a demand-side revenue-linked blanket licensing fee to incorporate publisher content into its AI search engine, Scout. Compensation is allocated back to publishers from separate company-specific royalty pools based on whether content is retrieved, grounded, or displayed with attribution.

## Key facts

- Yahoo became the first licensee of The Internet Licensing Collective (ILC), formerly known as Really Simple Licensing (RSL), to power its AI search engine Scout.
- Over 3,000 publishers are currently participating in the ILC collective.
- Yahoo pays a blanket licensing fee tied to demand-side revenue, which scales as product revenue grows.
- ILC structures payouts through segmented royalty pools separated by AI licensee rather than pooling all revenue into a single fund.
- Compensation is determined by allocation rules factoring in whether content is used for grounding, retrieval, or displayed in an AI response, as well as whether source citations are provided.
- A report from Mather Economics cited by Digiday estimated that collective licensing could generate publisher compensation equal to 15% to 35% of AI company revenue.
- The organization behind ILC also maintains the RSL Protocol, a machine-readable standard publishers use in robots.txt to set terms like pay-per-crawl or pay-per-inference fees.

## Insight

The deal marks a transition from bilateral, flat-fee AI licensing contracts toward collective, value-based clearinghouses using segmented royalty pools, which prevent high-value vertical content from being diluted by high-volume general web crawls.

## Implication

If collective licensing models gain traction across other AI developers, publishers may no longer need individual bespoke agreements to monetize automated crawling, while smaller frontier entrants gain a standardized path to access web publisher data legally.

## Why it matters

One-off licensing deals have historically favored top-tier media conglomerates, leaving mid-tail and independent publishers uncompensated. A collective royalty framework paired with machine-readable protocols could establish a repeatable commercial standard for AI crawling and retrieval.
